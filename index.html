Land, water, salt

Laos has no coastline — so its salt comes from underground.

Landlocked and far from any sea, Laos has drawn its salt from underground brine for generations. Across Vientiane Province, families have pumped brine into open ponds, let the sun and wind do the work, and raked the salt that reaches the rest of the country — a craft older than any factory.

Double Spoon Salt continues that work at our factory in Ban Somsavanh, Xaythany District, Vientiane Capital. We take the same underground brine this region has relied on for generations and turn it into the salt that reaches home kitchens, pool decks, and construction sites across Laos — produced here, by people from here.

Food registration

Our iodized table salt (ເກືອໄອໂອດິມ) is registered with the Lao Ministry of Health's Food and Drug Department under the Law on Food No. 33/NA, registration number ອຍ 1-01-08826-0001, valid to 31 July 2028.

The certificate confirms this product — 1 kg bags — is manufactured at our factory in Ban Somsavanh, Xaythany District, by ໂຮງງານຜະລິດເກືອໂຄກສະອາດ, the registered enterprise behind Double Spoon Salt. It's a formal registration step, not a health claim — by Lao law, this certificate can't be used to advertise health benefits or disease prevention.

Inside the factory

Modern equipment, careful hands

Brine becomes food-grade salt through washing, weighing, and sealed packaging — not just sun and time.

Wash tanks agitate the salt to rinse it clean before it's packed.

Screw conveyors move brine and salt through the line with less handling.

Workers wear gloves at the sorting and packing line.

Automatic scales and sealers pack each bag to the same weight.

People and place

Work that stays in Xaythany

Local hands, not a distant supply chain

Double Spoon Salt runs on people from the villages around our factory in Xaythany District. Every bag that leaves our gate is work done close to home, for people who live here.

Dried by sun, not by heavy processing

Our ເກືອຕາກ — literally "sun-dried salt" — is raked from open ponds after the sun and wind do the work, rather than pushed through heavy industrial processing. We depend on the same land and water our neighbors do, and we intend to keep it worth depending on.
